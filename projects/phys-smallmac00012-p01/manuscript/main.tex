\title{Order-three unistochastic matrices form a monoid: exact compression of measured qutrit dynamics}

\author{Xiaojian Zeng}
\email{xiaojianzeng@foxmail.com}
\affiliation{PBC School of Finance, Tsinghua University, 100190, China}
\date{September 13, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
An order-three unistochastic matrix has entries
$B(U)_{ij}=|U_{ij}|^2$ for a unitary $U\in U(3)$.  Whether the product of
two such matrices is always unistochastic is the three-dimensional instance
of a previously stated monoid conjecture.  We prove that it is.  The proof
has three ingredients.  First, when either factor is orthostochastic, an
exterior-algebra construction produces three certificate vectors whose norms
are the link lengths in the exact qutrit unistochasticity criterion and whose
sum vanishes.  Second, the standard phase parametrization of a generic
$3\times3$ unitary makes its squared-modulus matrix an affine chord between
two orthostochastic endpoints.  Third, the Heron witness for
unistochasticity is concave along this chord after multiplication by an
arbitrary bistochastic matrix.  Endpoint nonnegativity therefore propagates
to the entire chord, while compactness includes all singular charts.  In the
corresponding qutrit experiment, arbitrary unitary controls separated by a
complete, unrecorded basis measurement have exactly the same full transition
channel as one same-dimension unitary control followed by measurement.  The
result is exact and finite dimensional; it uses neither an ancillary
dimension nor an approximation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

For a unitary matrix $U\in U(d)$, the matrix of transition probabilities

\begin{equation}
 B(U)_{ij}=|U_{ij}|^2
 \label{eq:defB}
\end{equation}

is bistochastic.  Matrices admitting a representation of the form
Eq.~\eqref{eq:defB} are called unistochastic.  Their geometry is already
nontrivial in order three: they form a proper, nonconvex subset of the
Birkhoff polytope, but membership is decided exactly by a triangle-closure
condition \cite{Bengtsson2005}.  Unistochastic matrices also describe the
classical transition laws obtained when a finite quantum system is prepared
and measured in a fixed basis around a unitary evolution.

Rajchel-Mieldzio\'c \emph{et al.}\ introduced bracelet matrices, an outer
approximation to the unistochastic set, and conjectured that bracelet
matrices form a monoid under ordinary multiplication
\cite{RajchelMieldzioc2022}.  They proved closure under multiplication by a
factorisable bistochastic matrix.  In order three the bracelet and
unistochastic sets coincide, so their conjecture specializes to the question

\begin{equation}
 A,C\in\Ud \quad\Longrightarrow\quad CA\in\Ud.
 \label{eq:question}
\end{equation}

The known factorisable-multiplier result does not cover every
orthostochastic matrix in order three \cite{PoonTsing1987}, and the previously
proved circulant case is also a strict specialization.  Generalized or
dilated unistochastic representations \cite{Nechita2023,Barandes2026} enlarge
the realization space and therefore do not decide the same-size question
\eqref{eq:question}.

This paper proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:question} in full.  The central observation is
that the boundary-to-interior geometry of $\Ud$ is compatible with a concave
membership witness.  An exterior-algebra certificate first establishes
closure when one factor is orthostochastic.  The usual three-angle,
one-phase parametrization of $U(3)$ then expresses a generic unistochastic
matrix as a chord between two such endpoint matrices.  Along that chord, the
order-three Heron witness has a negative-semidefinite quadratic coefficient.
This turns endpoint closure into closure for arbitrary factors.

We keep the physical and mathematical statements separate.  The theorem is
about exact $3\times3$ matrices.  Its physical corollary concerns a fixed
qutrit basis, complete dephasing before and after each control, and the
entire channel rather than one chosen input distribution.  It does not say
that two controls can be generated by one time-independent Hamiltonian, and
it does not extend to dimension four.

\section{Measured qutrits and the order-three criterion}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$ be a fixed basis of
$\mathbb C^3$, and define complete dephasing in this basis by

\begin{equation}
 \Delta(X)=\sum_{j=0}^2 |j\rangle\!\langle j|X|j\rangle\!\langle j|.
 \label{eq:dephase}
\end{equation}

For $U\in U(3)$, set

\begin{equation}
 \Phi_U=\Delta\circ\Ad_U\circ\Delta,
 \qquad \Ad_U(X)=UXU^\dagger.
 \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}

We use column probability vectors, so $p'=B(U)p$.  If $V$ follows $U$ and
the intermediate measurement record is discarded, the transition matrix is
$B(V)B(U)$.

Write $\Bd$ for the set of nonnegative $3\times3$ matrices whose rows and
columns each sum to one, $\Ud=\{B(U):U\in U(3)\}$, and
$\Od=\{B(Q):Q\in O(3)\}$.  To state the exact order-three membership test,
define for $M\in\Bd$

\begin{equation}
 L_i(M)=\sqrt{M_{i0}M_{i1}},\qquad i=0,1,2,
 \label{eq:links}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{align}
 h(M)={}&2\!\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq2}L_i^2L_j^2
        -\sum_{i=0}^2L_i^4.                        \label{eq:hdef}
\end{align}

The qutrit criterion of Ref.~\cite{Bengtsson2005} can be written as

\begin{equation}
 M\in\Ud
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 2\max_i L_i\leq L_0+L_1+L_2
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h(M)\geq0.
 \label{eq:qutritcriterion}
\end{equation}

For completeness, the first equivalence follows by attempting to make the
first two columns of a unitary orthogonal.  After the first column is chosen
real and nonnegative, the condition is

\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=0}^2 L_i e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_i}=0,
 \label{eq:phasetriangle}
\end{equation}

which is possible exactly when three segments of lengths $L_i$ close.  The
first two columns are then orthonormal; their one-dimensional orthogonal
complement supplies the third column, whose moduli follow from the row sums
of $M$.  This argument includes a saturated triangle and zero-length links.
The second equivalence follows from the Heron factorization

\begin{align}
 h(M)={}&(L_0+L_1+L_2)(-L_0+L_1+L_2)\notag\\
 &\times(L_0-L_1+L_2)(L_0+L_1-L_2).
 \label{eq:heron}
\end{align}

We shall also use four coordinates for $\Bd$:

\begin{equation}
 M=\begin{pmatrix}
 a&b&1-a-b\\
 c&d&1-c-d\\
 1-a-c&1-b-d&a+b+c+d-1
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:fourcoord}
\end{equation}

Direct substitution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdef} gives the polynomial identity

\begin{equation}
 h(M)=4abcd-\bigl(a+b+c+d-1-ad-bc\bigr)^2.
 \label{eq:hcoord}
\end{equation}

No square root or division occurs in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hcoord}, which is useful
on the boundary of the polytope.

\section{An exterior certificate at orthostochastic endpoints}
\label{sec:exterior}

We begin with a multiplier class larger than the previously used
factorisable subset.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Lemma 1.}
\emph{If $A\in\Ud$ and $C\in\Od$, then $CA\in\Ud$.  By transposition, the
same conclusion holds if the right factor is orthostochastic.}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Proof.}
Choose $P\in U(3)$ and $Q\in O(3)$ such that

\begin{equation}
 A_{kj}=|P_{kj}|^2,\qquad C_{ik}=Q_{ik}^2.
 \label{eq:realizers}
\end{equation}

For the first two columns of $P$, and for every output row $i$, define
$x_i,y_i\in\mathbb C^3$ by

\begin{equation}
 (x_i)_k=Q_{ik}P_{k0},\qquad (y_i)_k=Q_{ik}P_{k1}.
 \label{eq:xy}
\end{equation}

Use the convention that the Hermitian inner product is antilinear in its
first entry, and form

\begin{equation}
 z_i=\bigl(\langle x_i,y_i\rangle,\,x_i\wedge y_i\bigr)
 \in \mathbb C\oplus\Lambda^2\mathbb C^3.
 \label{eq:zdef}
\end{equation}

The complex Lagrange identity yields

\begin{align}
 \|z_i\|^2
 &=|\langle x_i,y_i\rangle|^2+\|x_i\wedge y_i\|^2\notag\\
 &=\|x_i\|^2\|y_i\|^2
 =(CA)_{i0}(CA)_{i1}.                              \label{eq:lagrange}
\end{align}

Thus $\|z_i\|$ is exactly the link length $L_i(CA)$.

The scalar components sum to zero because the first two columns of $P$ are
orthogonal:

\begin{equation}
 \sum_i\langle x_i,y_i\rangle
 =\sum_k\overline{P_{k0}}P_{k1}\sum_iQ_{ik}^2=0.
 \label{eq:scalarcancel}
\end{equation}

For each $k<\ell$, the corresponding exterior components also cancel:

\begin{align}
 \sum_i\bigl[(x_i)_k(y_i)_\ell-(x_i)_\ell(y_i)_k\bigr]
 &=(P_{k0}P_{\ell1}-P_{\ell0}P_{k1})
   \sum_iQ_{ik}Q_{i\ell}=0.                       \label{eq:wedgecancel}
\end{align}

Consequently $z_0+z_1+z_2=0$.  The norm triangle inequality gives

\begin{equation}
 L_i(CA)=\|z_i\|\leq\sum_{j\ne i}\|z_j\|
 \quad(i=0,1,2),                                  \label{eq:normtriangle}
\end{equation}

so Eq.~\eqref{eq:qutritcriterion} proves $CA\in\Ud$.

The construction can also be read as a direct nonnegative certificate.  If
$g_{rs}=\langle z_r,z_s\rangle$, then $z_2=-z_0-z_1$ and

\begin{align}
 h(CA)
 &=4\bigl[g_{00}g_{11}-(\operatorname{Re}g_{01})^2\bigr]\notag\\
 &=4\bigl[g_{00}g_{11}-|g_{01}|^2
          +(\operatorname{Im}g_{01})^2\bigr]\geq0. \label{eq:gramcert}
\end{align}

The last inequality is the Gram determinant inequality plus a real square.
Finally, $(AC)^T=C^TA^T$ and both the unistochastic and orthostochastic sets
are invariant under transposition.  This proves the reversed-factor
statement. \hfill$\square$

The proof never assumes positive entries.  In particular, zero-link and
saturated products are already included and require no limiting phase
formula.

\section{The orthostochastic chord of a qutrit factor}
\label{sec:ckm}

The next ingredient moves from orthostochastic endpoints to an arbitrary
unistochastic factor.  A generic $3\times3$ unitary, modulo row and column
rephasings, has the standard three-angle, one-phase form
\cite{ChauKeung1984}

\begin{equation}
 U_{\mathrm{CKM}}(\delta)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 c_{12}c_{13}&s_{12}c_{13}&s_{13}e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta}\\
 -s_{12}c_{23}-c_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{\mathrm{i}\delta}
 &c_{12}c_{23}-s_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{\mathrm{i}\delta}
 &s_{23}c_{13}\\
 s_{12}s_{23}-c_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{\mathrm{i}\delta}
 &-c_{12}s_{23}-s_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{\mathrm{i}\delta}
 &c_{23}c_{13}
 \end{pmatrix},                                   \label{eq:ckm}
\end{equation}

where $s_{jk}=\sin\theta_{jk}$ and $c_{jk}=\cos\theta_{jk}$.  The row and
column phases do not change squared moduli.  The squared-modulus matrix
$C(\xi)=B(U_{\mathrm{CKM}})$ depends on the remaining phase only through
$\xi=\cos\delta$, and the dependence is affine.  Explicitly,

\begin{equation}
 \frac{dC}{d\xi}
 =\kappa\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\1&-1&0\\-1&1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \kappa=2s_{12}c_{12}s_{13}s_{23}c_{23}.          \label{eq:chorddirection}
\end{equation}

It follows that

\begin{equation}
 C(\xi)=\frac{1+\xi}{2}C(+1)+\frac{1-\xi}{2}C(-1),
 \qquad -1\leq\xi\leq1.                         \label{eq:chord}
\end{equation}

At $\xi=\pm1$ one may take $\delta=0$ or $\pi$; the representative
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ckm} is then real and unitary, hence orthogonal.  Thus every
generic member of $\Ud$ lies on an affine chord with endpoints in $\Od$.

For later use, it is enough that this construction holds on the generic
dense chart.  Indeed, generic unitaries are dense in $U(3)$, and the map
$U\mapsto B(U)$ is continuous.  Moreover, $\Ud$ is compact, because it is
the continuous image of compact $U(3)$, and hence it is closed.  These facts
will include collapsed chords and all other singular charts without dividing
by a vanishing mixing angle.

\section{Concavity and the monoid theorem}
\label{sec:main}

Fix an arbitrary $A\in\Bd$ and let $C(t)$ move affinely in the direction
Eq.~\eqref{eq:chorddirection}.  For $M(t)=C(t)A$, the first row is constant,
while the second and third rows move oppositely.  Therefore the four
coordinates of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fourcoord} can be written as

\begin{equation}
 a(t)=a,\qquad b(t)=b,\qquad
 c(t)=c+tr,\qquad d(t)=d+ts.                       \label{eq:affinecoords}
\end{equation}

Because $M(t)$ is bistochastic throughout the physical chord,
$a\geq0$, $b\geq0$, and $a+b\leq1$.  Substitution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:affinecoords} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hcoord} shows that the
coefficient of $t^2$ in $h(M(t))$ is

\begin{align}
 K
 &=4abrs-\bigl[(1-b)r+(1-a)s\bigr]^2\notag\\
 &=\begin{pmatrix}r&s\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 -(1-b)^2&ab+a+b-1\\
 ab+a+b-1&-(1-a)^2
 \end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}r\\s\end{pmatrix}.              \label{eq:K}
\end{align}

The two diagonal entries of the displayed symmetric matrix are
nonpositive, and its determinant is

\begin{equation}
 4ab(1-a-b)\geq0.                                  \label{eq:detK}
\end{equation}

The matrix is therefore negative semidefinite, so $K\leq0$.  We have proved
the following statement, including boundary values of $a$ and $b$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Lemma 2.}
\emph{For every $A\in\Bd$, the function $\xi\mapsto h(C(\xi)A)$ is concave
on every qutrit CKM chord.}

\medskip
We can now state the main result.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Theorem 1.}
\emph{The order-three unistochastic set is a monoid under ordinary matrix
multiplication.  Equivalently, for every $A,C\in\Ud$, one has $CA\in\Ud$.}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Proof.}
First suppose that $C$ is represented in a generic CKM chart, and write it as
$C(\xi)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.  Lemma~1 gives

\begin{equation}
 h(C(-1)A)\geq0,\qquad h(C(+1)A)\geq0,             \label{eq:endpoints}
\end{equation}

because both endpoint matrices are orthostochastic.  Lemma~2 and concavity
then imply

\begin{align}
 h(C(\xi)A)
 &\geq \frac{1-\xi}{2}h(C(-1)A)
       +\frac{1+\xi}{2}h(C(+1)A)\geq0.            \label{eq:concavitybound}
\end{align}

The product $C(\xi)A$ is bistochastic, so the exact criterion
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qutritcriterion} makes it unistochastic.

For arbitrary $C=B(V)\in\Ud$, choose generic unitaries $V_n\to V$.  The
preceding argument gives $B(V_n)A\in\Ud$ for every $n$, and
$B(V_n)A\to CA$.  Closedness of $\Ud$ gives $CA\in\Ud$.  The identity matrix
is unistochastic, completing the monoid statement. \hfill$\square$

The proof is specific to order three in two independent ways.  The CKM
phase gives a one-dimensional affine chord, and the three-link condition is
both necessary and sufficient for unistochasticity.  In order four, bracelet
conditions cease to be sufficient and the unistochastic set is not closed
under arbitrary products \cite{Bengtsson2005,RajchelMieldzioc2022}.

\section{Constructing the compressed control}
\label{sec:construction}

The theorem is existential at the level of $A$ and $C$, but the
order-three criterion gives a direct construction of a unitary realizing the
product.  Given $M=CA$, compute the three lengths in Eq.~\eqref{eq:links}.
Because $h(M)\geq0$, choose phases $\alpha_i$ that close the triangle in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasetriangle}.  Define the first two columns of $W$ by

\begin{equation}
 W_{i0}=\sqrt{M_{i0}},\qquad
 W_{i1}=\sqrt{M_{i1}}e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_i}.       \label{eq:Wcolumns}
\end{equation}

Column normalization follows from bistochasticity and their inner product
vanishes by construction.  Choose the third column as either unit vector in
their one-dimensional orthogonal complement.  The resulting square matrix
is unitary.  Its row normalization gives

\begin{equation}
 |W_{i2}|^2=1-|W_{i0}|^2-|W_{i1}|^2
 =1-M_{i0}-M_{i1}=M_{i2},                          \label{eq:thirdmodulus}
\end{equation}

and hence $B(W)=M$.  Saturated and zero-link cases are handled by choosing
collinear or arbitrary zero-link phases before taking the orthogonal
complement; no cosine-law division is required.

We now translate this construction back to the measured qutrit.  From
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dephase}--\eqref{eq:channel}, for every operator $X$,

\begin{equation}
 \Phi_U(X)=\sum_{i,j=0}^2 B(U)_{ij}
 \langle j|X|j\rangle\,|i\rangle\!\langle i|.     \label{eq:channelmatrix}
\end{equation}

Therefore

\begin{equation}
 \Phi_V\circ\Phi_U=\Phi_W
 \quad\text{whenever}\quad B(W)=B(V)B(U).         \label{eq:channelequality}
\end{equation}

This is equality of linear maps, not agreement on one input probability
vector.  It consequently remains true after tensoring both sides with the
identity channel on any finite-dimensional reference system.  The
intermediate record is discarded, and $W$ acts on the same three-level
system.  The conclusion does not assert $W=VU$, does not preserve an
intermediate outcome, and does not impose a common Hamiltonian or a control
energy bound.

Associativity immediately allows the matrix statement to be iterated over
any finite product in $\Ud$.  Operationally, each iteration still assumes
the same fixed basis and complete dephasing at the specified interfaces.

\section{Exact checks and use of generative AI}
\label{sec:methods}

The proof above is analytic and does not rely on a finite search.  Two
accompanying SymPy programs provide reproducible algebraic checks.
The first verifies the complex Lagrange identity, all cancellation
contractions, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gramcert}, including strict and zero-entry
examples.  The second expands Eqs.~\eqref{eq:hcoord}, \eqref{eq:K}, and
\eqref{eq:detK}, verifies the CKM endpoints symbolically, and completes an
independent exact interior example to a unitary $W$.  The environment and
commands are specified in the accompanying reproducibility file.  Numerical
sampling used during exploration is not evidence for the universal theorem.

OpenAI Codex (a GPT-5-based coding agent, OpenAI; accessed September 2026)
was used in literature triage, proof exploration, symbolic-checking code,
referee-style computational rechecks, and manuscript drafting.  Its role was
to accelerate algebraic exploration and to expose the argument to distinct
symbolic and numerical checks.  The named author must critically review the

\section{Conclusion}

Order-three unistochastic matrices are closed under ordinary multiplication.
The proof combines an exterior-algebra endpoint certificate with the affine
phase geometry of a qutrit unitary and a negative-semidefinite concavity
form.  It resolves the order-three specialization of the bracelet-monoid
conjecture without restricting either factor to the previously known
factorisable or circulant classes.

For a fixed qutrit basis, the result gives exact compression of two arbitrary
unitary-and-dephasing steps into one unitary-and-dephasing step at the level
of the full channel.  This is a finite-dimensional structural statement.  It
does not supply a higher-dimensional closure theorem, an implementation by a
single time-independent Hamiltonian, or an experimentally costed control
protocol.  Those distinctions are essential when using the monoid property
as a statement about physical dynamics.
